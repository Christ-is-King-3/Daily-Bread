1. Who is Jesus?

Jesus Christ is God the Son, who became truly human. The Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are three distinct persons, and there is one God.

Read John 1:1–18 (ESV)

John begins before the birth of Jesus. He says that the Word was with God and that the Word was God. Hold those statements together. The Word is distinct from the Father and shares fully in the one divine being. In verse 14, the Word becomes flesh. The one who was with the Father in the beginning enters our world as Jesus Christ.

At Jesus’ baptism, the Son comes up from the water, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father speaks from heaven. Later in John’s Gospel, Thomas meets the risen Jesus and addresses him as Lord and God. These passages help us see why Christians confess that Jesus is God and why Jesus can pray to the Father. Matthew 3:13–17 · John 20:24–29

Try this. Read John 1:1 and 1:14 again. Write one sentence on who Jesus is and one on what he did by becoming human.

Check your understanding. What happens at Jesus’ baptism?

The Son is baptised, the Holy Spirit descends, and the Father speaks. Matthew presents them together in the same scene. John 1 helps us see how the Son can be distinct from the Father and fully God.

2. Why did Jesus die and rise again?

Jesus died for our sins and rose bodily from the dead. Through him, God offers forgiveness and the hope of resurrection.

Read 1 Corinthians 15:1–22 (ESV)

Paul is writing to a church where some say the dead are never raised. He reminds them of the gospel they received. Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to witnesses. The death addresses our guilt before God. The burial and appearances anchor the message in what happened to Jesus.

In verses 17–22 Paul follows the argument through. If Christ has remained dead, believers are still in their sins. Since Christ has risen, his resurrection is the first of the harvest. His rising is the promise of more to follow. Those who belong to him can look forward to being raised. In Mark’s Gospel, Jesus has already told his disciples that he came to give his life as a ransom for many. Mark 10:35–45

Try this. Read 1 Corinthians 15:17–22 slowly. Tell God one thing you need forgiveness for, and thank him for the hope that comes through Jesus.

Check your understanding. Why does the resurrection matter for believers?

Paul says that if Christ has stayed dead, believers are still in their sins. Since Christ has risen, they have hope of forgiveness and of being raised with him. Read verses 17 and 20–22 together.

Read further
  • What Is the Gospel? — D. A. Carson. An exposition of 1 Corinthians 15.
  • The Cross of Christ — John Stott. A study of what Christ accomplished through his death.

3. How do I begin following Jesus?

Turn to God and trust Jesus Christ. God saves by grace through faith and calls those he saves to a life of obedience.

Read Mark 1:14–20 (ESV)

Jesus announces that God’s kingdom has drawn near and calls people to repent and believe the good news. Repentance means turning away from sin and turning towards God. Faith means trusting Christ himself. Mark immediately shows Jesus calling fishermen to follow him. Their response involves a change of direction in ordinary life.

In Ephesians 2, Paul describes people who were dead in sin and whom God made alive with Christ. Grace is God’s undeserved kindness to us. Salvation is his gift, received through faith. Paul then says we are created in Christ for good works. Obedience grows out of the life God gives us. In Acts 2, new believers are baptised and join a community devoted to teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayer. Ephesians 2:1–10 · Acts 2:37–47

Try this. Take a few minutes to pray honestly about where you need to turn to God. Read the rest of Mark 1. If you have a church nearby, speak with a minister about following Jesus and what baptism means.

Check your understanding. Where do good works fit into salvation?

Read Ephesians 2:8–10 in order. Salvation comes as God’s gift through faith. In Christ, God then gives us a new life in which to do the good works he has prepared for us.

4. How do I read the Bible?

Read each passage in its setting and in the wider story of God’s promises fulfilled in Christ.

Read Luke 24:13–35 (ESV)

Two disciples are walking away from Jerusalem. They know Jesus was crucified and have heard about the empty tomb, yet their hopes have collapsed. The risen Jesus takes them through Moses and the Prophets to show why the Messiah had to suffer before entering his glory. The Scriptures of Israel give the cross and resurrection their setting. At the same time, every passage has its own voice. A psalm gives words for praise or lament. A Gospel tells what Jesus did and said. An apostolic letter addresses particular churches and questions.

Start with Mark. Read a whole scene before deciding what one verse means. Ask who is speaking, what just happened, and what the writer wants you to see about God or his people. Then ask what response fits the passage. In Mark 2:1–12, the healing answers the question raised earlier in the story about Jesus’ authority to forgive sins.

Try this. Read Mark 2:1–12 and underline the question in verse 7. Read to the end and write how Jesus answers it.

Check your understanding. Why read the whole scene before applying one verse?

The events explain one another. In Mark 2, the visible healing confirms Jesus’ authority to do something the crowd cannot see: forgive sins.

Read further
  • How to Read the Bible — BibleProject. A reading plan introducing the different kinds of writing in Scripture.
  • On Christian Doctrine, Book I — Augustine of Hippo. An early Christian account of how reading Scripture leads to love of God and neighbour.

5. How do I pray?

Jesus teaches us to speak to our Father about his name and kingdom, our daily needs, our forgiveness, and our need for help against evil.

Read Matthew 6:5–15 (ESV)

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus contrasts prayer offered for an audience with prayer addressed honestly to the Father. The Father already knows what his children need, and Jesus still teaches them to ask. The prayer begins with God’s name, kingdom and will. Then it gives us words for food, forgiveness and protection. The shared words “our” and “us” remind us to pray for others even when we are alone.

Jesus returns to forgiveness in verses 14–15. He takes seriously how people who ask God for mercy treat those who have wronged them. You can begin with Jesus’ words and then name a concrete need, sin or person before God. When words are hard to find, Romans 8:26–27 speaks of the Spirit helping us in our weakness.

Try this. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly. After asking for daily bread, name one person besides yourself who needs God’s provision today.

Check your understanding. What does Jesus’ prayer teach us to put first?

Jesus begins with the Father’s name, kingdom and will. Daily needs have a real place within that larger purpose.

Read further
  • The Message of the Sermon on the Mount — John Stott. Stott’s exposition of the sermon in which Jesus teaches this prayer.
  • A Catechism — Church of England. The historic Anglican explanation of what Christians ask for in the Lord’s Prayer.

More Christian foundations

Continue in order, or open the question you need today. The lessons build from life in the Church to Christian hope, the Bible’s history and the history of the Church.

  1. Who is the Holy Spirit?

    The Holy Spirit is God, dwelling in believers, making Christ known to them and giving them power to put sin to death.

    Read Romans 8:9–17 (ESV)

    Paul describes Christian life as life in the Spirit. The Spirit dwells in believers and is the Spirit of the God who raised Jesus. The same passage speaks of the Spirit of Christ. This helps explain why the Spirit’s work draws believers towards the risen Lord. Paul says that by the Spirit believers put to death the deeds of the body. He then speaks of adoption, the Spirit’s work in bringing them to call on God as their Father.

    The promises of adoption and inheritance sit alongside suffering with Christ in verse 17. When life is hard, the Spirit’s presence can be seen in dependence on God, a desire to turn from sin, and love that grows over time. Galatians 5:22–25 names the fruit the Spirit produces.

    Try this. Pick one fruit named in Galatians 5. Ask God for help to practise it in one specific relationship this week.

    Check your understanding. What does the Spirit of adoption enable believers to do?

    Romans 8:15–16 says believers cry out to God as Father, and the Spirit bears witness that they are God’s children.

    Read further
    • The Holy Spirit: Agent of Salvation — David Garner. Traces the Spirit’s work in bringing believers into the benefits of Christ’s saving work.
  2. Why do Christians need a church?

    Christ calls believers into a community where they hear his Word, pray, worship and learn to serve one another.

    Read Acts 2:37–47 (ESV)

    In Jerusalem at Pentecost, Peter’s hearers ask how to respond to the message that Jesus is Lord and Christ. Those who receive the message are baptised and devote themselves to the apostles’ teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayers. Their devotion has material consequences. They share possessions to meet needs and eat together with gladness. Luke shows faith taking visible shape in a community.

    Church life helps you learn to receive correction, ask for prayer and carry responsibilities alongside other people. When you visit a church, listen for how it teaches Scripture and speaks about Jesus. Ask how newcomers can meet people, read the Bible with others and get support when life is difficult.

    Try this. Read Acts 2:42–47 before visiting a service. Pick one thing the Jerusalem believers devoted themselves to and ask how that church practises it.

    Check your understanding. What followed baptism in Acts 2?

    The believers joined a community devoted to teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayer. Their shared life also involved meeting practical needs.

    Read further
    • Worship and Evangelism in Pre-Christendom — Alan Kreider. A historical lecture on worship and the formation of converts in the early centuries.
    • First Apology, chapter 67 — Justin Martyr. A second-century witness to Sunday worship and care for people in need.
  3. What is baptism?

    Baptism is the act Jesus commanded in which a person is baptised with water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.

    Read Romans 6:1–11 (ESV)

    Paul asks whether believers should go on sinning so that grace may increase. He points them to their baptism. They have been baptised into Christ’s death and buried with him so that they may walk in newness of life. His argument ties baptism to what Christ has done and to the life that follows. In Matthew 28:18–20, the risen Jesus brings baptism and teaching together in his commission to make disciples.

    The Church of England baptises adults who profess faith and also baptises infants, with parents and godparents making promises on their behalf. Many Baptist churches baptise people after their own profession of faith. Anglicans and Baptists also differ in how they explain what baptism does. Your minister can explain your church’s practice and help you understand a baptism you may have received when you were young.

    Try this. Read Romans 6:4. Ask a minister how your church teaches the connection between baptism and the new life Paul describes.

    Check your understanding. What question is Paul answering by bringing up baptism?

    He is answering whether grace gives believers reason to continue in sin. Their baptism points to Christ’s death and resurrection and a life turned towards God.

    Read further
    • Holy Baptism: Liturgy — Church of England. Read the promises and the words used when the Church of England baptises.
    • Articles of Religion, Article XXVII — Church of England. The historic Anglican statement on baptism, including the baptism of infants.
  4. What is Holy Communion?

    Jesus gave his Church bread and the cup to receive in remembrance of him and to proclaim his death until he comes.

    Read 1 Corinthians 11:17–34 (ESV)

    Paul repeats what he received about the night Jesus was betrayed. Jesus connects the bread with his body and the cup with the new covenant in his blood. Paul brings those words into a painful situation. Some Corinthians are eating their fill while others go hungry, and their gatherings humiliate those who have little. Self-examination in this passage includes facing how we treat the people with whom we share the Lord’s Table.

    Holy Communion, the Lord’s Supper and the Eucharist are names you may hear for this meal. Anglican teaching speaks of faithful reception of Christ’s body and blood in a heavenly and spiritual manner. Other churches describe Christ’s presence differently and have different practices about who receives. If you are visiting or preparing for baptism, ask the minister how to take part.

    Try this. Read verses 20–22 and 33–34 alongside verses 23–26. Consider whether someone in your congregation is being overlooked and how you could welcome them.

    Check your understanding. Why does Paul discuss the way the Corinthians treat each other?

    The meal proclaims the death of the Lord who gave himself for them. Humiliating poorer believers contradicts the fellowship they gather to express.

    Read further
    • Articles of Religion, Article XXVIII — Church of England. The historic Anglican explanation of the Lord’s Supper.
    • First Apology, chapters 65–67 — Justin Martyr. An early description of the Eucharist within Christian worship.
  5. How can I know God has forgiven me?

    The promise of forgiveness rests on Jesus Christ. When you sin, bring it into the light and confess it to God.

    Read 1 John 1:5–2:2 (ESV)

    John writes to believers about fellowship with God. To walk in the light is to live honestly before the God who sees us. John includes himself when he says that claiming to be without sin is self-deception. He gives a promise to those who confess their sins: God is faithful and just to forgive and cleanse them. The next verses name Jesus as the advocate who represents sinners before the Father.

    Assurance grows as you take Christ at his word. A troubled feeling can prompt honest examination, but it cannot tell you by itself whether God has forgiven you. Confession also has practical consequences. Where you have harmed someone, take responsibility and make amends where possible. When the same struggle keeps recurring, speak to a mature Christian or minister who can pray and help you respond truthfully.

    Try this. Pray through 1 John 1:9 and 2:1. Name your sin plainly, then name the promise and the person John directs you to.

    Check your understanding. Why can a Christian confess sin without despairing?

    John grounds forgiveness in God’s faithfulness and Christ’s work. Jesus remains the believer’s advocate with the Father.

    Read further
    • Assurance — The Gospel Coalition. A theological essay on God’s promises and the believer’s confidence in forgiveness.
  6. How do I change after becoming a Christian?

    Christian growth means learning, by the Spirit, to put away old ways of living and practise the new life Christ gives.

    Read Ephesians 4:17–32 (ESV)

    Paul describes growth with the picture of taking off old clothes and putting on new ones. Then he names specific changes. The person who used to steal should work honestly and have something to share. The person who used words to tear down should learn to speak in ways that build others up. The passage moves beyond stopping a harmful behaviour. It gives the believer a positive action that serves someone else.

    Christians often call this growth in holiness sanctification. The passage addresses a community, so habits affect the people around us. A person learning truthful speech may need to confess an old lie, make a correction and practise honesty the next time the truth costs something. Verse 32 grounds kindness and forgiveness in what God has already done in Christ.

    Try this. Choose one of Paul’s examples. Write down the old habit, the new action and the person that new action could help.

    Check your understanding. What does Paul tell a person who has been stealing to do with honest earnings?

    The person should work honestly and share with anyone in need. The new habit redirects their attention towards a neighbour.

    Read further
    • The Doctrine of Sanctification — Fred Zaspel. Explains how being set apart in Christ gives rise to practical growth in holiness.
    • The Radical Disciple — John Stott. A short book about growing in dependence and Christlikeness.
  7. What do love and forgiveness require of me?

    Jesus commands us to love God and our neighbour. That love takes shape in concrete mercy, even when it costs us something.

    Read Luke 10:25–37 (ESV)

    A lawyer asks Jesus who counts as a neighbour. Jesus tells of a wounded man passed by two people and helped by a Samaritan. The Samaritan treats his wounds, takes him somewhere safe and pays for further care. At the end, Jesus changes the focus of the question. He asks who acted as a neighbour. The answer points to mercy that crosses a boundary the lawyer might otherwise have used to limit responsibility.

    In Matthew 18:21–35, Jesus addresses another costly expression of love. The forgiven servant refuses to show mercy to someone who owes him. Christians remember what God has forgiven when they deal with personal wrongs. Forgiveness can go alongside naming harm, seeking justice and maintaining safe boundaries. Restored trust calls for truth and a change in conduct over time.

    Try this. Name one person whose need you have noticed. Decide on one practical act of mercy you can carry out this week.

    Check your understanding. How does Jesus turn the lawyer’s question around?

    He moves from asking who qualifies for help to asking who showed mercy. The lawyer must consider how he himself will act.

    Read further
    • On Christian Doctrine, Book I — Augustine of Hippo. Augustine treats love of God and neighbour as central to understanding and living Scripture.
    • The Message of the Sermon on the Mount — John Stott. Jesus’ teaching on love of enemies, mercy and righteousness in Matthew 5–7.
  8. What went wrong with God’s good creation?

    God made the world good and gave people dignity as his image-bearers. Human rebellion brought shame, alienation and death.

    Read Genesis 3:1–24 (ESV)

    Read Genesis 3 after chapters 1 and 2. There is already a world worth loving, work worth doing and a relationship with God before the serpent speaks. The temptation begins by questioning God’s word and his goodness. The man and woman take what God has forbidden, hide when he comes and shift blame when he questions them. The scene shows what sin does to trust, responsibility and human relationships.

    Paul sets Adam and Christ alongside one another in Romans 5:12–21. Sin and death enter through Adam, while grace and righteousness come through Jesus Christ. This helps put the gospel inside the Bible’s whole story. God’s good creation matters, humanity’s rebellion is serious, and the grace of Christ addresses a problem larger than any one bad habit.

    Try this. Read Genesis 3:8–13. Notice what changes about how the man and woman relate to God and to each other.

    Check your understanding. Why read Genesis 1 and 2 before Genesis 3?

    Those chapters establish the goodness of what God made. Genesis 3 shows that sin damages something good that preceded it.

    Read further
    • The Beginning and End of All Things — Edward W. Klink III. Traces creation, fall, redemption and new creation across Scripture.
  9. What do I do when I suffer or doubt?

    Bring grief and unanswered questions to God. Christian hope gives you a reason to pray while the trouble is still present.

    Read Psalm 13 (ESV)

    The psalm begins with repeated questions about how long God will seem distant. The writer names inward sorrow and outward danger, then asks God to look and answer. Only then does he speak of trust in God’s steadfast love. The prayer itself gives room for distress and trust together. It ends in confidence while leaving the reader without a report of how the outward events turned out.

    In Mark 9:14–29, a father brings his suffering child to Jesus and asks for help with his unbelief. His prayer gives words to someone who wants to trust and struggles to do so. You can speak to God plainly and ask someone at church to pray with you. If your struggle is an intellectual question, write down exactly what you are asking and read the passage or evidence with someone willing to listen.

    Try this. Pray Psalm 13 in its three movements. Name the trouble, ask God for help, and put one reason for trust into your own words.

    Check your understanding. Where in Psalm 13 does the writer express trust?

    Trust comes after the psalmist voices his grief and asks for help. The prayer makes room for all three.

    Read further
    • Walking with God through Pain and Suffering — Timothy Keller. A pastoral and theological treatment of suffering and Christian endurance.
  10. What happens when Christ returns?

    Christ will return, raise the dead, judge justly and bring his people into God’s renewed creation.

    Read Revelation 21:1–8 (ESV)

    John sees a new heaven and new earth and the holy city coming down from God. The centre of the vision is God dwelling with his people. Death, mourning and pain are removed. The passage also speaks of judgement. God’s promise of a world healed from evil goes together with his promise to deal justly with evil.

    In 1 Corinthians 15:42–58, Paul describes resurrection as God raising and transforming the body. He ends by urging believers to be steadfast in their work for the Lord. That final instruction helps us see the point of Christian hope. What believers do in service to Christ matters in the present because of the future he promises.

    Try this. Read Revelation 21:3–5 beside 1 Corinthians 15:58. Which promise would help you keep serving Christ in a difficult situation?

    Check your understanding. What does John say is at the heart of the new creation?

    God will dwell with his people. The end of death and grief belongs to that promise of life with him.

    Read further
    • Surprised by Hope — N. T. Wright. An Anglican New Testament scholar explores bodily resurrection and its implications for the Church’s life today.
  11. How do I share my faith?

    Speak honestly about Christ, and give people reasons for your hope with gentleness and respect.

    Read 1 Peter 3:8–17 (ESV)

    Peter writes to Christians who may suffer for doing good. He tells them to honour Christ as Lord and be ready to explain their hope. Gentleness, respect and a good conscience belong to his instruction. The witness of words and the witness of conduct meet when Christians answer people who misunderstand them or treat them unfairly.

    A simple explanation can begin with who Jesus is, what he did and why it changed the way you live. Ask what your friend understands by words like sin, grace or resurrection. Where you do not know the answer, say so and return to the question after reading. Jesus’ commission in Matthew 28:18–20 also includes teaching people to obey what he commanded, so sharing faith includes helping others take their next steps.

    Try this. Write three ordinary sentences about your hope in Jesus. Share them in a conversation where the other person is willing to listen, and make time to hear their questions.

    Check your understanding. What attitude belongs with explaining Christian hope?

    Peter calls for gentleness, respect and a good conscience. The way a Christian answers should fit the Lord whose hope they are explaining.

    Read further
    • Christian Mission in the Modern World — John Stott and Christopher J. H. Wright. A discussion of evangelism and practical love within Christian mission.
  12. How did Christians get the books of the Bible?

    Christians inherited Israel’s Scriptures and came to recognise a collection of apostolic writings as the New Testament.

    Read 2 Peter 3:14–18 (ESV)

    Peter refers to Paul’s letters and speaks about people twisting them along with the other Scriptures. This shows early Christians already treating some apostolic writing as Scripture. It gives us a glimpse of a process that continued as writings were read publicly, shared among churches and received over time. Apostolic connection, the books’ message and their reception in the Church all matter to the history.

    The New Testament has 27 books across the main Christian traditions. The Old Testament collections used by Protestants, Roman Catholics and Orthodox churches differ in which books they include. The Church of England’s Article VI lists books read for instruction as well as the books it uses to establish doctrine. Those differences help explain why a Bible supplied by one church may have more books than one supplied by another.

    Try this. Find the contents page in your Bible. Locate the four Gospels, Acts and Paul’s letters, then follow the discussion of Paul’s letters in 2 Peter.

    Check your understanding. What does 2 Peter show us about Paul’s letters?

    They already circulated and were treated as Scripture. The passage gives early evidence of their authority within the Church.

    Read further
    • The Biblical Canon — Michael J. Kruger. An evangelical scholar’s explanation of apostolic connection, reception and the character of the books.
    • Articles of Religion, Article VI — Church of England. The historic Anglican account of canonical Scripture and additional books read for instruction.
  13. What happened after the apostles?

    Early Christians spread the gospel, gathered for worship, handed on apostolic teaching and used creeds to state publicly what they believed.

    Read Acts 28:16–31 (ESV)

    Acts ends with Paul in Rome, teaching about Jesus under house arrest. In the generations that followed, Christian communities continued to gather. In his second-century First Apology, Justin Martyr describes Sunday Scripture readings, instruction, prayer, Communion and an offering for people in need. Read him alongside Acts 2. You can compare his second-century description with Luke’s account of Jerusalem without assuming every local church followed an identical pattern.

    In AD 325 the Council of Nicaea affirmed that the Son shares the Father’s divine being. Constantinople in 381 reaffirmed the Nicene faith and clarified the Church’s confession of the Holy Spirit. Chalcedon in 451 gave a further account of Christ as one person, fully divine and fully human. Hurtado’s historical research places devotion to Jesus in the earliest years of the movement. Philippians 2:5–11 lets you examine that earlier evidence directly.

    Try this. Read Justin’s chapter 67 after Acts 2:42–47. List two continuities you can recognise and one further question you would ask a historian.

    Check your understanding. What were Christians at Nicaea trying to clarify?

    They were giving a public account of the Son’s relation to the Father. The Son’s full divinity was already a question Christians approached through Scripture and worship.

    Read further
    • Early Devotion to Jesus (2011) — Larry Hurtado. A peer-reviewed study of the emergence of devotion to Jesus among the earliest believers.
    • First Apology, chapter 67 — Justin Martyr. A primary source for Christian worship in the second century.
    • Christological Controversies in the Early Church — Coleman Ford. An introduction to the disputes and councils that shaped the Church’s language about Jesus.
  14. Why are there different churches and denominations?

    Christian traditions developed through differences over doctrine, worship and church authority, as well as language, geography and politics.

    Read Ephesians 4:1–16 (ESV)

    Paul urges Christians to guard the unity of the Spirit in peace. He names one Lord, one faith and one baptism, and then he describes different gifts equipping believers to grow into maturity. Unity includes speaking the truth in love. That gives you a way to approach church differences: learn what people believe, ask why it matters to them, and represent their beliefs fairly.

    The division between the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches developed over centuries, with 1054 a conventional landmark. The sixteenth-century Reformation brought further divisions within Western Christianity, including the English Reformation and the development of Anglicanism. The Coptic and Armenian churches belong to the Oriental Orthodox family, whose separation from other churches developed after Chalcedon in 451. That earlier history matters when people use “Orthodox” to describe different Christian traditions. For a first visit, what matters most is understanding how the congregation confesses Christ, reads Scripture and lives together. You can learn the history as your questions get more specific.

    Try this. Compare the Nicene Creed with the statement of faith or service booklet from a church near you. Ask a minister what the church shares with others and which differences affect worship or church life.

    Check your understanding. How does Paul connect unity and truth?

    Paul calls believers to preserve unity in peace and to speak the truth in love as they grow together into Christ.

    Read further
    • The Nicene Creed — Church of England. A shared touchstone for many Christian traditions, provided here in Anglican liturgical wording.
    • Oriental Orthodox Churches — Geevarghese Mar Osthathios. An account of the Oriental Orthodox churches by an Indian Orthodox bishop.
    • East–West Schism — Encyclopaedia Britannica. Historical context for the gradual separation of Eastern and Western churches.
    • Reformation — Encyclopaedia Britannica. Historical context for the sixteenth-century reform movements in Western Christianity.
How this course is ordered

The first five move through Christ’s person and work, our response, and the habits of Bible reading and prayer. The remaining lessons start with the Spirit and the shared life of the Church. They then address questions new believers meet as they grow and the history behind the Bible and Christian traditions.

  • Basic Christianity — John Stott. Starts with who Christ is and what he has done, then explains how to respond.
  • Why Your Church and Family Need Catechesis — J. I. Packer and Gary A. Parrett. Grounds the teaching of new Christians in belief, worship and conduct.
  • A Catechism — Church of England. Connects the Creed, commandments, prayer, baptism and Holy Communion.
